## Abstract

Generative models like Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), Diffusion Models (DMs), and Generative Adversarial Networks have shown strong capabilities in realistic image generation, but often lack interpretability and fine-grained control. To address these limitations, Causal VAE-DM is proposed as a hybrid model that combines causal inference with generative modeling to enhance transparency and controllability. By integrating the representational capability of VAEs with the high-quality image synthesis of DMs, this method enables precise editing of specific image attributes while preserving realism. Experiments on two datasets demonstrate that Causal VAE-DM outperforms existing causal generative models in both interpretability and feature-editing performance.

**Index Terms** — Trustworthy generative model, Image modification, Causal inference, Variational autoencoders, Diffusion models

## I. Introduction

Generative models, powered by deep learning and reinforcement learning, have gained widespread attention in various fields, like robotics, autonomous vehicles, and medical treatments. However, with more generative models used in critical applications, the requirement of the trustworthiness of the models becomes paramount. Causal inference provides a systematic framework to enhance transparency by explicitly modeling the dependencies between variables using causal graphs. While traditional causal methods rely on structured data with well-defined relationships, generative models typically work with unstructured inputs like images, where extracting causal relationships is more challenging.

For images, causal factors might relate to complex visual features or hidden attributes, such as lighting, texture, or object positioning, which are not easily represented by simple variables. To obtain structured data from images, causal representation learning is proposed to transform raw data, such as images, into structured variables that can be used in causal models. Causal representation learning provides deeper insights into how observed features in images impact specific outcomes or decisions.

Generating high-quality images in causal generative models is challenging due to the complexity of accurately modeling causal relationships while maintaining the realism and fidelity of generated data. In generative models, variational autoencoders (VAEs) could obtain representations easily but it is hard to produce high-quality images due to their smooth loss function. Diffusion Models (DMs) could generate images with high quality, but it is hard to get their representations as they rely on a sequence of noise-to-signal transitions.

To avoid the drawbacks of these two methods, this paper proposes a **Causal VAE-DM** (Causal Variational Autoencoder-Diffusion Model) method to generate high-quality images with causal relationships. The integration of the Causal VAE-DM reduces dependency on extensive real-world data collection, resulting in significant cost savings. Additionally, Causal VAE-DM accelerates development by enabling the generation of high-quality, causally consistent synthetic data. The main contributions are as follows:

1. Synthetic data generated by Causal VAE-DM is both realistic and aligned with real-world causal relationships, enhancing interpretability and robustness.
2. The method generates diverse and high-quality images that capture various environmental conditions, which reduces dependence on real-world data and lowers costs.
3. By introducing causal factors to image generation, Causal VAE-DM improves interpretability and control, which supports transparent decision-making in applications like autonomous vehicle vision.

## II. Related Works

Causal inference goes beyond mere correlations to uncover true cause-and-effect relationships using frameworks like do-calculus and structural causal models (SCMs), enabling counterfactual analysis through methods such as NOTEARS and LLM-based approaches. This approach is particularly valuable in generative modeling, where integrating causal reasoning with disentangled representation learning enhances both interpretability and trustworthiness. By incorporating causal structures into models like CausalVAE and causal GANs, the generation process becomes transparent, allowing controlled interventions and ensuring that synthetic images faithfully reflect real-world causal relationships. Such advancements are crucial for sensitive applications in healthcare and autonomous systems.

## III. Causal Variational Autoencoders with Diffusion Model

The proposed Causal VAE-DM method combines causal inference with VAE and DM to generate counterfactual images. Through the causal graph and the evaluation of the results, the changes in the generated images could be explained. In this section, the architecture, learning process, and evaluation process of the Causal VAE-DM are presented.

### A. Model Architecture

#### 1) Overall Structure

The structure of the Causal VAE-DM is illustrated in Figure 1. This diagram illustrates a structure for a causal image generative model, where an encoder processes the input image, and a causal graph represents relationships between factors. The output includes intermediate and counterfactual images generated through a causal latent decoder and a conditional DM to observe effects from specific causal changes.

In this structure, the causal matrix is presented by SCM, which converts the relationship in the graph to a matrix. This causal matrix **A** is introduced to encode the causal representations. The mechanism of this process is factorized as:

$$\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{z} + \epsilon = (I - \mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T})^{-1}\epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \tag{1}$$

where $\epsilon$ represents independent Gaussian exogenous factors, $\mathsf{T}$ denotes the transpose of a matrix, and $\mathbf{z}$ is the latent variable, which is an unobserved factor underlying observed data, processed in the mask layer.

Since the model's input consists of unstructured data, it is essential to convert the unstructured data into a structured form. In the proposed model, the latent variable $\mathbf{z}$ serves as the required structured data, encapsulating the underlying causal factors that influence the visual features in the images. A structured encoding process is employed to derive $\mathbf{z}$, allowing it to capture these causal relationships effectively.

#### 2) Confounding Factors

Confounding factors are variables that influence both the cause and the effect within a study, leading to potential biases in estimating causal relationships. These factors create a spurious association that can obscure the true causal effect or suggest a relationship where none exists. For example, in image generation tasks, confounders such as background patterns or lighting conditions can simultaneously affect both the input features (cause) and the target outputs (effect).

A classifier is introduced after obtaining the initial latent representation to address the presence of confounding factors. The mechanism of the classifier is represented as:

$$\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W} \cdot \mathbf{x}_0 + \epsilon_{\text{classifier}} \tag{2}$$

$$\mathbf{k}_{\text{classifier}} = \mathbf{M} \cdot \mathbf{z} + \eta_{\text{classifier}} \tag{3}$$

where $\mathbf{W}$ is the weight matrix, $\mathbf{x}_0$ is the input image, $\epsilon_{\text{classifier}}$ denotes independent Gaussian noise in the classifier, $\mathbf{k}_{\text{classifier}}$ is the output of the classifier, and $\mathbf{M}$ and $\eta_{\text{classifier}}$ are additional parameters. The classifier utilizes a fully connected network and applies cross-entropy as the loss function. The loss function $\mathcal{L}_{CE}$ is defined as:

$$\mathcal{L}_{CE} = -\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{sample}}} \sum_{c=1}^{C} l_{i,c} \log \hat{l}_{i,c} \tag{4}$$

where $N_{\text{sample}}$ is the number of samples, $C$ is the number of classes, $l_{i,c}$ is the true label, and $\hat{l}_{i,c}$ is the predicted probability that sample $i$ belongs to class $c$.

#### 3) Conditional Diffusion Model

The conditional DM iteratively refines noisy inputs into high-quality images using the VAE-generated intermediate image as a guide. It denoises images step-by-step, aligning outputs with $\mathbf{z}$, and uses causal factors to control specific image attributes, ensuring real-world causal consistency.

The **forward process** is:

$$q(x_{1:T} | x_0) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t | x_{t-1}) \tag{5}$$

$$q(x_t | x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I) \tag{6}$$

The **reverse process** is:

$$p(x_{0:T}) = p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_{t-1} | x_t) \tag{7}$$

$$p(x_{t-1} | x_t) = \mathcal{N}(\mu(x_t, t),\ \Sigma(x_t, t)) \tag{8}$$

where $x_i$ ($i = 1, 2, \ldots, T$) is the $i$-th input of the DM, $t$ represents the diffusion step, $\beta_t$ represents the variance schedule at time step $t$, $T$ represents the number of steps, $p$ is the target distribution, $q$ denotes a probability distribution, and $\mu(x_t, t)$ and $\Sigma(x_t, t)$ represent the mean and variance of the Gaussian distribution respectively.

In the conditional DM, the forward process is not affected by condition $y$:

$$\hat{q}(x_{1:T} | x_0, y) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(x_t | x_{t-1}) \tag{9}$$

However, $y$ guides the reverse process:

$$p(x_{0:T} | y) = p(x_T) \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_{t-1} | x_t, y) \tag{10}$$

### B. Model Learning

#### 1) Overall Learning Strategy

The learning process of the Causal VAE-DM is divided into training the VAE and training the conditional DM. The overall loss function is:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{DM}} + \mathcal{L}_{CE} \tag{11}$$

#### 2) Learning Strategy of VAE

The conventional VAE loss has two components: a reconstruction loss and a KL divergence term.

**Reconstruction loss:**

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{reconstruction}} = -\mathbb{E}_{q(z|x)}[\log p(x|z)] \tag{12}$$

**KL divergence:**

$$\mathcal{L}_{KL} = \int q(z|x) \log \frac{q(z|x)}{p(z)}\, dz \tag{13}$$

To ensure the identifiability of the adjacency matrix **A**, the learning guidance $u$ is used:

$$l_u = \mathbb{E}_X \| u - \sigma(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T} u) \|_2^2 \tag{14}$$

where $u$ is the label, $\sigma$ is a logistic function, and $\|\cdot\|_2^2$ represents the squared L2-norm.

$\mathbf{z}$ is regulated by:

$$l_z = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\phi} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} \| z_i - g_i(\mathbf{A}_i \circ z;\ \eta_i) \|_2^2 \right] \tag{15}$$

where $n$ is the number of causal factors, $g_i$ is the $i$-th element of a set of mild nonlinear and invertible functions $g$, $\circ$ denotes the elementwise product, and $\eta_i$ is the parameter of the function set $g$.

The full VAE loss in Causal VAE-DM is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{reconstruction}} + \mathcal{L}_{KL} + \gamma l_u + \lambda l_z \tag{16}$$

where $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ are the coefficients of the constraints $l_u$ and $l_z$.

#### 3) Learning Strategy of DM

For the conditional DM, the label $u$ guides the learning process. Given an image $x_0$, the loss function factorized with condition $y$ is:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DM}} = \mathbb{E}_{z \sim q_\psi(z|y,x_0)} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(x_{1:T}|y,z,u,x_0)} \log \frac{q_\phi(x_{1:T} \mid y, z, u, x_0)}{p_\psi(x_{0:T} \mid y, z, u)} \right] \tag{17}$$

### C. Model Intervention

With the loss function (11), the Causal VAE-DM obtains the desired causal latent. The intervention is deployed during testing to evaluate the performance of the model. The intervention process refers to actively changing or setting the value of a variable to observe how this manipulation affects other variables in the system.

The intervention process is commonly denoted by *do*-calculus, where an intervention is represented as $do(X = x)$, meaning that variable $X$ is set to value $x$ irrespective of its natural causes.

## IV. Experiment

The proposed Causal VAE-DM model is evaluated through comprehensive experiments conducted on two datasets. The evaluations are designed to demonstrate the model's interpretability, effectiveness in learning causal relationships, and capability to generate accurate and trustworthy counterfactual images. The experiments are carried out on a high-performance server configured with Ubuntu 20.04 and equipped with two NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.

### A. Datasets

**MNIST:** The MNIST dataset comprises 70,000 grayscale images of handwritten digits (0–9) at 28×28 pixels, divided into 51,000 for training, 9,000 for validation, and 10,000 for testing, serving as a standard benchmark for image recognition and deep learning.

**Flow Dataset:** The Flow Dataset contains 7,000 RGBA images (96×96 pixels) illustrating a ball interacting with fluid in a broken vessel, split into 5,100 for training, 900 for validation, and 1,000 for testing. In this dataset, the ball size influences water height, which affects water flow, while the hole size also impacts the water flow.

### B. Experiment Details

The Causal VAE-DM is trained on each dataset individually. In the MNIST dataset, the digit itself is identified as the confounder in the causal relationship — when the cause is changed, the digit also changes. To address this, a classifier is incorporated. No observed confounder is found in the Flow dataset, so no classifier is used for that model.

### C. Experimental Results

#### 1) Causal Graphs

Figure 2 shows heatmap visualizations of the causal matrix **A** at the 100th epoch. In Figure 2(a) (MNIST), the yellow region confirms that the factor *thickness* influences the factor *intensity*. In Figure 2(b) (Flow), the matrix encodes the causal chain: *ball size* → *hole* → *water height* → *water flow*. Both results confirm the model correctly learns the causal relationships.

#### 2) Results on the MNIST Dataset

For MNIST, the label *thickness* ranges from 1 to 5.8 and *intensity* from 67 to 255. The Causal VAE-DM results for $do(\text{Thickness} = 2)$ and $do(\text{Thickness} = 4)$ correspond to the ideal intervention: a decrease in thickness leads to a corresponding decrease in intensity and vice versa. In contrast, the conventional CausalVAE shows unclear intensity changes, and at higher thickness values, background errors appear that are absent in the Causal VAE-DM outputs.

#### 3) Results on the Flow Dataset

Both Causal VAE-DM and conventional CausalVAE can learn the causal relationships in the Flow Dataset. When ball size is changed, water height also changes, and water flow is subsequently influenced. However, the Causal VAE-DM produces better image detail than the conventional CausalVAE.

#### 4) Results Analysis

Mean Absolute Error (MAE) is used to evaluate the model's control over latent factors.

**Table I: MAE Comparison on MNIST and Flow Datasets**

| Dataset | Intervention | Causal VAE-DM | Conventional CausalVAE |
|---------|-------------|---------------|------------------------|
| MNIST | $do(\text{thickness} = 2)$ | 0.06 | 1.01 |
| MNIST | $do(\text{thickness} = 4)$ | 0.081 | 0.845 |
| Flow | $do(\text{ball size} = 15)$ | 0.023 | 16.6553 |
| Flow | $do(\text{water height} = 65)$ | 0.021 | 19.1042 |
| Flow | $do(\text{hole} = 3)$ | 0.091 | 8.7898 |
| Flow | $do(\text{water flow} = 11)$ | 0.011 | 9.7236 |

The MAE of the Causal VAE-DM is substantially lower than that of the Conventional CausalVAE across all interventions. Additionally, conventional Conditional DMs are not able to change effects by changing causes; only Causal VAE-DM shows preferable performance in controlling the latent factors (MAE on thickness: Causal VAE-DM 0.06/0.081 vs. Conventional Conditional DM ~0.144).

---

## V. Conclusion

This study introduced Causal VAE-DM, a trustworthy architecture for generating counterfactual images. The model effectively disentangles and manipulates latent factors, as evidenced by lower MAE scores and superior image quality compared to conventional methods. The experimental results underscore its value in advancing the development of interpretable and reliable generative models. Additionally, with results on different causal graphs, the Causal VAE-DM demonstrates adaptability and consistent performance across various environments.

In future work, the Causal VAE-DM will be enhanced to identify and disentangle a broader range of causal factors. Furthermore, its application will be extended to engineering domains, providing innovative solutions to practical problems and advancing the integration of causal learning in real-world systems.
