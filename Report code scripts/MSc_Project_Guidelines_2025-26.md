## Conducting Your MSc Project

All projects must be completed in Glasgow.

It is very important that you put sufficient time into your project from the start — especially if you depend on facilities or software that are heavily used by others. Careful planning is essential.

You are expected to show initiative and work independently. Project supervisors provide guidance, but formulating and undertaking the work is your responsibility and forms part of the assessment.

### Preliminary Report

Before writing your preliminary report, agree your project objectives with your supervisor. These must indicate the expected outcomes; your project will be assessed against how well you achieve them.

The preliminary report must include:

- The objectives
- A brief description of the project and its background
- A Gantt chart identifying the main tasks and a schedule
- Resources needed, when they are required, and how they will be obtained
- An initial list of key references

The contents must be agreed with your supervisor before commencing detailed work.

### Collaboration

Your project is to be an individual effort. However, with some projects it would be unrealistic to expect one student to achieve the aim alone. You may be required to collaborate with others, in which case your supervisor will allocate individual work packages, each with its own objectives.

### Meetings and Lab Book

You are expected to work full-time on your project from the end of May until the end of August. During this period, you should have regular meetings with your supervisor (e.g. every 1–2 weeks). You are strongly encouraged to keep a record of your work in a **Lab Book**, to be discussed during supervisor meetings. Be aware that your supervisor may not be continuously available over summer.

> **Visa note:** If you require a Tier 4 visa, the University is obliged to confirm that you are engaged with studies and meeting regularly with your first supervisor.

## How to Write a Technical Project Report

### Abstract

All reports start with an abstract, which contains a summary of the project: its aim, what was done, and what was achieved. The abstract should be approximately **100–250 words**.

### Report Structure

```
Abstract
Contents
1  Introduction
2  Body of the Report
3  Discussion
4  Conclusions
   References
A  Appendices
B  University's Plagiarism Statement
```

---

### 1. Introduction

Many students put off writing until too late — this is a serious mistake, as the report typically accounts for a large fraction of the assessment. Start early and think about the report throughout your project.

The introduction should explain the background to the project and the reasons why the work was considered worthwhile, leading clearly to the **aims** of the project. Be specific.

#### 1.1 Mechanical Aspects

- **Length:** Approximately 40–50 pages (10,000–15,000 words) for the body of the report. Appendices are additional.
- **Format:** Word-processed, A4, 12-point Times, single line spacing, pages numbered, margins of at least 25 mm.
- **Front cover:** Title, name(s), matriculation number(s), supervisor name(s).
- **Abstract:** ~100–250 words on the page following the cover.
- **Table of contents:** On the page after the abstract.
- **Body sections:** Each starting on a new page, numbered. Figures and tables must have captions and be numbered.

---

### 2. Body of the Report

#### 2.1 Structure and Sections

The structure depends on the nature of the project. Two common types:

**Research Projects**

- **Theory** — Essential background theory relevant to the project. Demonstrate understanding; do not repeat standard textbook material.
- **Experimental Techniques** — Account of experimental or numerical techniques. Include details necessary to continue or replicate the work.
- **Results** — Describe at an appropriate level of detail to support conclusions. Leave lengthy tables to an appendix.

**Design, Build and Test Projects**

- **Specification** — Develop a vague description into a firm specification against which the final product will be judged.
- **Possible Strategies** — Briefly evaluate approaches considered and justify the chosen one.
- **Implementation** — Describe the final product, focusing on key features. For software, describe high-level structure in the main text and include a full listing as an appendix.
- **Testing** — Equivalent to the 'Results' section of a research project.

#### 2.2 Style of Writing

Assume the reader is a well-educated, graduate engineer but not an expert on the project topic. They are familiar with general concepts up to Level 3 but not with specialised higher-level courses.

> **Watchword: SUMMARISE!**

- Write in correct English with appropriate paragraphing, sentence length, and punctuation.
- Use the passive voice: write *'The voltage was measured'* rather than *'I measured the voltage'*.
- Define all abbreviations on first use, e.g. *'serial peripheral interface (SPI)'*. Provide a list of abbreviations if many are used.
- Do not write material you do not understand.

The quality of English is assessed as part of the report.

#### 2.3 Precision

- Quote numerical results to an appropriate number of significant figures.
- Analyse uncertainties in your results.
- Avoid vague statements such as *'The gain was quite accurate'*. Instead, write: *'The accuracy of the gain was estimated to be ±2%, limited by the tolerance of the resistors.'*

#### 2.4 Figures and Tables

- All figures and tables must have informative, numbered captions.
- Graph axes must have scales, titles, and units.
- Use error bars on data points to indicate uncertainty.
- All text in figures must be legible (roughly the same size as the main text).
- Tables must have appropriate headings with units. Avoid lengthy tables; consider whether a graph or separate data file would be clearer.

#### 2.5 References

References must be cited with **sequential numbers in square brackets** where used in the text — not just listed at the end. Example usage:

> *'Fitzmaurice and Hand [5] showed that...'*
> *'The median is an appropriate estimator for this signal [5].'*

Avoid direct quotations where possible. Illustrations from data sheets are acceptable quotations and must be referenced in the figure caption.

**Required information by reference type:**

| Type | Required Information |
|---|---|
| Journal paper | Author(s), title, journal name, volume, pages, year |
| Book | Author(s), title, edition, publisher, year (chapter/page if partial) |
| Data sheet | Company, title, edition, date |
| Web page | Author(s)/organisation, title, full URL, date of viewing |

**Note on web references:** Web pages, especially anonymous sites such as Wikipedia, cannot always be verified. Use them only as a starting point. Reports whose references are all or mainly from web sources will be penalised.

**Plagiarism:** The University of Glasgow takes plagiarism (including use of generative AI such as ChatGPT) very seriously, and this may lead to heavy penalties. See: [University of Glasgow — Plagiarism](https://www.gla.ac.uk/myglasgow/sld/plagiarism/)

---

### 3. Discussion

The discussion is your opportunity to impress an expert with your depth of understanding.

- **Research projects:** Provide a logical argument from experimental observations to final conclusions. Evaluate results thoroughly, compare with other work critically, and explain any differences.
- **Design-and-build projects:** Assess the performance of your product against the specification, showing strengths and weaknesses.

Never write *'The results were as expected'* unless you have already described exactly what was expected.

---

### 4. Conclusions

Every report must have conclusions built on the discussion. Include:

- A summary of the main achievements
- What has been learnt as a result of the project
- An assessment of outcomes against the original aims
- Explanation of any unfulfilled aims
- A review of the project plan

**Suggestions for Further Work** — Almost every project leaves ideas for the future. Examiners are impressed by intelligent suggestions, as they show genuine understanding of the project.

## Appendices

Use appendices for supporting material that is relevant but detailed in nature, so as not to interrupt the flow of ideas in the body of the report. Appendices are usually **lettered** (A, B, C…) rather than numbered.

- Appendices are **not included** in the word count.
- Assessors are not obliged to read them — **do not put vital points in an appendix**.
- Include your **project plan** and **interim reports** as appendices.
- Large tables of results, long programs, and data sheets may be better supplied as separate data files.

## University's Plagiarism Statement

*(Taken from Section 32 of the University Calendar)*

The University's degrees and other academic awards are given in recognition of a student's personal achievement. All work submitted for assessment is accepted on the understanding that it is the student's own effort.

**Plagiarism** is defined as the submission or presentation of work, in any form, which is not one's own, without acknowledgement of the sources. Plagiarism includes inappropriate collaboration with others.

**Self-plagiarism** includes using work already submitted for assessment at this or any other institution.

Work may be considered plagiarised if it consists of:
- A direct quotation
- A close paraphrase
- An unacknowledged summary of a source
- Direct copying or transcription

If information or ideas are obtained from any source, that source must be acknowledged according to the appropriate convention. Any direct quotation must be placed in quotation marks and the source cited immediately.

Plagiarism is considered an act of fraudulence and an offence against the University Code of Student Conduct. Alleged plagiarism will be investigated and dealt with appropriately, whether before or after graduation.

The University reserves the right to use plagiarism detection systems, which may be externally based.

## MSc Project Marking Guide

### Overview

| Component | Weight |
|---|---|
| Written report/dissertation | 60% |
| Conduct throughout the project | 20% |
| Poster presentation | 20% |

### Written Report Assessment

- **Supervisors** assess both the written report and overall project conduct, plus the poster presentation.
- **Moderators** assess the written report and poster independently of the supervisor.

Written reports go to the Head of Discipline (or nominated representative) for arbitration if:
- The overall grade is 20 (A3) or higher
- There is a significant difference between supervisor and moderator grades
- The supervisor and moderator cannot agree a grade
- There are special circumstances affecting the project outcome
- The overall grade is 8 (E1) or lower

Staff will not make allowances for medical circumstances when marking; these are considered at the exam board.

### Project Poster

- Presented at the **Poster Conference** and assessed by the project supervisor.
- Staff will not make allowances for medical circumstances when assessing the poster.
